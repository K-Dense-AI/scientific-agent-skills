# Nature and Science Writing Style

**Reviewed: 2026-10-01.** Writing suggestions here are not mandatory paragraph
counts or evidence of acceptance. Nature, Science, Nature Communications,
Science Advances, and PNAS have distinct author instructions.

## Nature: verified submission conventions

Primary sources:

- https://www.nature.com/nature/for-authors/initial-submission
- https://www.nature.com/nature/for-authors/formatting-guide

Nature permits reasonable flexibility at initial submission. Submit a readable
PDF for a LaTeX-authored manuscript before acceptance, with numbered lines.
Its Article summary is a **referenced paragraph**, ideally no more than 200
words, understandable beyond the specialty. Do not apply the citation-free
abstract rule used by many other journals to this summary.

Methods belong in the main manuscript file following the figure legends for
Nature's specified organization. They form part of the online article; they are
not automatically Supplementary Information. Extended Data and Supplementary
Information are distinct categories. For production, use the editor's current
figure guide rather than a cached universal DPI or column-width rule.

The page/word relationships in Nature's formatting guide describe typical
published layouts. They are not a universal 3,000-word cap or five-page initial
submission limit. Other article types and Nature Portfolio journals differ.

## Science and related journals

Start at https://www.science.org/content/page/instructions-authors and select
the exact journal and article type. Fresh access to Science's instructions was
blocked in this audit. Consequently this guide does not assert current abstract
limits, reference-title omissions, or Methods placement for Science. Obtain the
current instructions before adapting a manuscript.

PNAS has a separate author center at https://www.pnas.org/author-center. Do not
transfer Nature's summary format to PNAS's abstract/significance components.

## Writing for readers outside the specialty

Begin with the specific scientific problem, then explain the unresolved issue
and how the study addresses it. Define unavoidable technical terms. Broad
interest should emerge from supported implications, not sweeping priority or
clinical-translation claims.

A useful summary sequence is:

1. Context accessible to the target readership.
2. The exact knowledge gap.
3. The principal result and supporting approach.
4. What the result changes, with the relevant limits.

For Nature's summary, include only essential numbers and abbreviations. In
results and legends, retain quantitative detail, uncertainty, and units.

`assets/examples/nature_abstract_examples.md` (relative to the skill root)
contains illustrative placeholder-based examples, not scientific findings.
Never convert a plausible teaching example into a cited result or reference.

## Introduction

Move from the problem to the research question and contribution. The required
length follows the article type, not a fixed three-to-five-paragraph formula.
Select the prior work needed to establish the gap and distinguish the advance;
do not force a percentage reduction in jargon or remove necessary definitions.

Avoid fabricated historical statistics or claims such as “all of sequence
space” when evaluation covers a restricted benchmark. Qualify the domain of
applicability and give the evidence that supports broader implications.

## Results and Discussion

Organize Results around findings rather than a diary of experiments. Each
finding should link to its analysis, figure, and relevant controls. State
observed effects and uncertainty before interpretation. Replication units,
exclusions, multiplicity, and missingness matter more than a stock significance
asterisk.

In the Discussion, distinguish observation, mechanistic inference, and
speculation. Compare alternative explanations and prior studies fairly.
Acknowledge limitations where they affect a claim, rather than using a generic
closing disclaimer. Do not assert an essential biological role from a single
knockout result without considering viability, off-target effects, rescue, and
other relevant explanations.

## Methods

Provide enough detail for interpretation and reproduction: design, independent
units, sample-size rationale, exclusions, blinding/randomization where relevant,
protocols, instrumentation, analysis, and software versions actually used.
Reference established methods while describing departures from them.

Report ethics and consent status accurately. Record required data/code
availability and any justified access restrictions; “available on request” is
not a universal alternative to a required repository deposition.

## Figures and references

Use an explanatory schematic when it helps readers understand the model or
workflow, alongside the data needed to evaluate it. A schematic is not a
substitute for evidence. Caption symbols, panels, units, scale bars, sample
sizes, error bars, and analyses. Preserve quantitative integrity and editable
artwork for production.

Use the target journal's reference style and verified bibliographic records.
For Nature, numbered superscript citations and article titles are appropriate.
Avoid invented example references with realistic titles, DOIs, or page ranges.
Use the published version when it supersedes the cited preprint, and check for
corrections or retractions before using a real paper as a model.

## Pre-submission review

- Is the significance understandable without overstating scope?
- Are quantitative and causal claims supported by the actual design and data?
- Does the summary follow this article type's citation and length rules?
- Are Methods, Extended Data, and Supplementary Information correctly separated?
- Are reporting, funding, contribution, interests, and availability statements complete?
- Does the PDF match the current initial-submission instructions?
